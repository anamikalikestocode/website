ai is not going to take your job

In 1930, Keynes, the smartest economist alive, predicted the average person would work 15 hours a week by 2030. He thought technology would make us so rich and satisfied that we'd barely need to work.

We are one year from his deadline. The average American works 41 hours a week.

He was wrong for the exact same reason everyone panicking about AI and jobs is wrong today.

He thought human desire had a ceiling. It doesn't and it never has.

Every generation thinks this time is different. During the industrial revolution, when the mechanical loom arrived in the early 1800s, workers smashed the machines in the streets. They weren't stupid. Their jobs actually were disappearing. But cheaper cloth created a market that hadn't existed before, which would eventually employ more people than hand-weaving ever did.

Same story with the Dot Com Bubble and the computer. The unemployment discourse today is exactly what you would have said in 1999 about a social media manager, UX designer or podcast producer.

Every single time, serious people looked at innovation and concluded that this wave was the one that would finally break the system.

Every single time, they were wrong for the same reason.

They measured the technology against the current shape of the economy. Not against the economy it was about to create.

Technology doesn't satisfy desire. It manufactures it. Nobody wanted to doomscroll before the smartphone existed. Nobody was frustrated because they couldn't watch brainrot for hours.

These desires didn't preexist the technology. Technology created them. And then those created desires became billion dollar industries with millions of people employed inside them.

Here's the deeper reason this keeps happening.

A homeless person doesn't think about leisure, they think about food. Once fed and warm, they start thinking about status, beauty, community, novelty, legacy. There is no natural end state here. Satisfying desire at one level generates desire at the next.

What this means for AI is that the right question isn't whether AI will do the jobs we have now (it will do many of them). The right question is what new categories of desire AI itself will generate, and what labor those desires will require.

One path here is arguing that with the rise of AI, more people are paying for things that are human. Handmade objects. Live performances. In-person experiences. I'm not sure how much I believe that. A trend I believe more in is how more people are starting companies than ever before; this itself is a revenue-generating desire that AI has opened up.

The new industries that will form around AI-generated desire mostly don't exist yet.

The economy literally cannot function without consumers.

Henry Ford understood this in 1914 when he doubled his workers' wages and his peers called him a socialist. His reasoning was completely ruthless: he wanted his workers to be able to afford the cars they were building.

AI labor displacement, if large enough to meaningfully reduce purchasing power, triggers a feedback loop the system is structurally motivated to prevent. Unemployment reduces demand. Reduced demand causes more layoffs. More layoffs reduce demand further. You end up with a technically productive system that has nobody to sell to. It collapses in on itself.

And then there's the political reality.

Every time in history that technology has concentrated gains at the top while displacing workers at scale, the political response has eventually been overwhelming (see: the labor movement, minimum wage laws, unemployment insurance). A future where AI enriches a few thousand people while everyone else can't pay rent is not a stable equilibrium. There's no strong reason that changes now.

AI is extraordinary in software and basically nowhere in the physical world.

This is the most underrated point in the entire conversation.

AI disruption to knowledge work is real. But the same systems cannot wire your house, fix your plumbing, perform surgery or build the data center they run on. The robotics and hardware problem is categorically different from the language modeling problem and it has resisted serious engineering effort for decades.

Actually, the AI boom itself has created one of the largest physical labor demands in recent American history. Data centers require human hands to build and maintain all of it. The investment currently flowing into physical AI infrastructure is staggering.

The most advanced software in human history is simultaneously creating enormous demand for physical skilled labor.

The technology that was supposed to replace everyone is currently employing a lot of people to build the rooms it lives in.

AI cannot be held responsible. Regulation will not allow it.

Here's something that almost never comes up in this conversation.

AI cannot hold a medical license. It cannot sign a legal contract in any meaningful sense. The entire infrastructure of accountability that underlies professional life — in law, medicine, finance, engineering — assumes human agents who can face consequences. This creates irreducible demand for human professionals in any domain where accountability matters.

An AI can produce a legal analysis of extraordinary quality. The lawyer who signs off on it takes on personal and professional liability for its contents. That signature is not a formality. It's the mechanism by which a client can seek recourse if something goes wrong. Unless someone figures out how to make an AI carry real consequences, the human in the loop is not temporary but a feature of those AI workflows.

Trust is not just about competence. We trust doctors partly because doctors can lose their licenses, face lawsuits, or go to prison. Those consequences align incentives in ways that capability alone cannot replicate.

So Please Stop Worrying About Your Corporate 9-5

Yes, AI will eliminate specific jobs held by specific people in specific places.

That part is real and worth saying clearly. History isn't a clean montage where disruption resolves itself and everyone lands on their feet. Whole regions and generations have been hit by previous waves and genuinely never recovered; not because the new jobs didn't exist, but because they existed somewhere else, for someone else, requiring a different skillset. The argument here isn't that AI will be gentle. It's that mass permanent unemployment is not something AI does to us automatically. It's what happens when the technology arrives and we fail to respond; with retraining, redistribution, policy and probably a lot more I haven't even begun thinking of.

But the version of the story where AI just ends employment as a concept does not exist. Where half the world has nothing to do and no income. That version requires you to believe that human desire has a ceiling. That the economy can survive without consumers. That robotics in the physical world has been solved. That democratic societies will sit quietly while a small class captures everything.

None of that has ever been true. There's no strong argument that it becomes true now.

My favorite argument is how Keynes was wrong because he thought satisfaction was an end state. But AI, for all of what it can do, has not found a way to make us want less.

So yeah, the fifteen-hour work week is not coming — not because AI will underdeliver. But because we will find something new to want, build a world to serve that desire, and employ ourselves in the process.

We always do.
